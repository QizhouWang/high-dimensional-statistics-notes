SECTION 01Introduction to Tail and Concentration Bounds

In this chapter, we study how to bound the probability that a random variable takes an extreme value or deviates far from its mean.

For example, we use the average height of 100 randomly selected people to estimate the citywide average. We want to know how likely this estimate is to differ from the true average by more than 2 cm. Tail and concentration bounds help us control this probability.

Definition · Tail Probability

A tail probability measures how likely a random variable is to take an extreme value, for example:

P(X≥t),P(X≤−t)

A tail bound gives an upper bound on such a probability.

Definition · Concentration Inequality

A concentration inequality bounds the probability that a random variable deviates from its mean or median. A two-sided bound around the mean takes the form:

P(|X−E[X]|≥t)≤b(t)

Here, t is the deviation threshold and b(t) bounds the probability of a deviation of at least t.

SECTION 02Classical Bounds

Setting and Goal

Assume X ≥ 0, t > 0, and k is a positive integer with a finite kth moment. We want to bound the tail probability P(X ≥ t).

Claim 1 · Controlling Tails through Moments

One way to control a tail probability is by controlling the moments of the random variable.[1] Controlling higher-order moments can give sharper tail bounds.[2] Specifically,

P(X≥t)≤E[Xk]tk

[1] “Controlling a moment” means knowing its value or an upper bound. If large values occur often, E[Xᵏ] must be large. Conversely, a small moment limits how often large values can occur.

[2] Higher powers amplify extreme values more strongly, so controlling higher-order moments gives more information about the tail. But a larger k does not automatically give a tighter bound: both E[Xᵏ] and tᵏ change. Choose the smallest bound among the available orders. For a general real-valued X, use absolute moments:

P(|X|≥t)≤infkE[|X|k]tk

The infimum is taken over positive integer orders with finite, known moments (or their available upper bounds).

Proof of Claim 1

If X ≥ t always holds, then Xᵏ ≥ tᵏ always holds, so E[Xᵏ] ≥ tᵏ. More generally, if X ≥ t holds with probability P(X ≥ t), then E[Xᵏ] ≥ tᵏ P(X ≥ t), because Xᵏ is at least tᵏ in those outcomes and is nonnegative in all other outcomes.

Example · Why This Contribution Is at Least tᵏ P(X ≥ t)

Imagine 100 observations, of which 10 satisfy X ≥ 5. Each of those 10 contributes at least 25 to the sum of squares, so their total contribution is at least 10 × 25 = 250. The other squares are nonnegative, so the average of all 100 squares is at least 250 / 100 = 2.5.

This illustrates the same rule: minimum contribution × proportion of outcomes. Here it is 25 × 0.1 = 2.5.

Applying this reasoning to the population expectation gives

E[Xk]≥tkP(X≥t)

Dividing by tᵏ > 0 proves Claim 1:

P(X≥t)≤E[Xk]tk

This inequality holds deterministically under the stated assumptions; it bounds the probability of a random event.

Example · k = 2: Variance Controls Deviations

The second moment E[X²] equals the variance only when E[X] = 0. For a general X, the variance is E[(X − E[X])²]: the average squared deviation from the mean.

Apply Claim 1 to the nonnegative variable |X − E[X]| with k = 2. This gives Chebyshev’s inequality:

P(|X−E[X]|≥t)≤Var(X)t2

For example, if an estimate has variance 1 cm², its probability of differing from its mean by at least 2 cm is at most 1 / 2² = 25%. A smaller variance gives a smaller bound at the same threshold.
