SECTION 01Markov, Chebyshev, and Chernoff Bounds

In the previous section, we introduced basic tail and concentration bounds to build an initial understanding. We now start with Markov’s inequality and develop several commonly used bounds, all based on the idea of controlling tails through moments that we derived earlier.

Setting

We want to bound the probability of a large value or a large deviation from the mean. The information available—such as a mean, variance, or moment generating function—determines which bound we can use.

Theorem 1 · Markov’s Inequality

For a nonnegative random variable X with a finite mean, and any t > 0,

P(X≥t)≤E[X]t

A small mean limits how often X can be large. This is the moment bound with k = 1.

Proof of Theorem 1

When X ≥ t, X contributes at least t to its expectation; all other contributions are nonnegative. Thus E[X] ≥ t P(X ≥ t). Divide by t.

Theorem 2 · Chebyshev’s Inequality

For a random variable X with mean μ = E[X] and finite variance, and any t > 0,

P(|X−μ|≥t)≤Var(X)t2

A small variance limits the probability of a large deviation from the mean.

Proof of Theorem 2

Apply Markov’s inequality to the nonnegative random variable Y = (X − μ)², using the threshold t². Its mean is E[Y] = Var(X), and Y ≥ t² is equivalent to |X − μ| ≥ t.

Claim · Both Bounds Are Sharp

Neither bound can be improved uniformly over all distributions satisfying its assumptions. This does not mean the bound is tight for every distribution.

Example · A Distribution That Attains Equality

Let t > 0 and 0 < p ≤ 1. Let X equal −t, 0, and t with probabilities p/2, 1 − p, and p/2, respectively. Its mean is 0 and its variance is pt², so

P(|X|≥t)=p=Var(X)t2

Chebyshev’s bound is exactly the true probability p. Any smaller bound at this same mean, variance, and threshold would fail for this distribution. Markov’s bound also attains equality: take a nonnegative variable equal to t with probability p and 0 otherwise.

SECTION 02Higher-Order Moment Bounds

Theorem 3 · Centered Moments

For any positive integer k with E[|X − μ|ᵏ] finite, and any t > 0,

P(|X−μ|≥t)≤E[|X−μ|k]tk

This extends Chebyshev’s inequality beyond the second moment.

Proof of Theorem 3

Apply Markov’s inequality to Y = |X − μ|ᵏ with threshold tᵏ. Higher powers make large deviations contribute more strongly to E[Y].

A higher order does not automatically give a smaller bound: both the moment and tᵏ change. Compare the available bounds and choose the smallest.

SECTION 03From Exponential Moments to the Chernoff Bound

Definition · Centered Moment Generating Function

Let μ = E[X]. The centered moment generating function is

ϕ(λ)=E[eλX−μ]

Assume there is a b > 0 such that ϕ(λ) is finite for every |λ| ≤ b. This is what it means for the MGF to exist in a neighborhood of zero.

Theorem 4 · Chernoff Bound

For any t > 0 and any 0 < λ ≤ b,

P(X−μ≥t)≤e−λtϕ(λ)

Choose λ to obtain the smallest upper bound. Equivalently,

logP(X−μ≥t)≤inf0<λ≤b{logϕ(λ)−λt}

Proof of Theorem 4

For λ > 0, the exponential is increasing. The event X − μ ≥ t is therefore equivalent to e^{λ(X−μ)} ≥ e^{λt}. Apply Markov’s inequality to Y = e^{λ(X−μ)}:

P(X−μ≥t)=P(eλX−μ≥eλt)≤E[eλX−μ]eλt

The inequality holds for every permitted λ, so we can take the infimum over λ. Taking logarithms gives the stated form.

Apply the same argument to −(X − μ) for the lower tail. Combining the two one-sided bounds gives a two-sided concentration bound.

Exponential moments are especially useful for sums of independent variables: the MGF of a sum factors into a product, making it easier to compute and optimize the bound.

SUPPLEMENTARYMoments and Moment Generating Functions

This section explains the intuition behind the tools used above.

Definition · What Does a Moment Measure?

The kth raw moment is E[Xᵏ]. Absolute moments E[|X|ᵏ] measure magnitude without cancellation; centered moments measure deviations from the mean. In particular, E[(X − μ)²] is the variance.

Definition · Why Use a Generating Function?

The exponential expansion collects all powers of X in one expression. If M(λ) = E[e^{λX}] is finite in a neighborhood of zero, then

M(λ)=1+λE[X]+λ22!E[X2]+λ33!E[X3]+⋯

The kth moment is the coefficient of λᵏ multiplied by k!. One function therefore organizes the entire sequence of moments. The centered MGF ϕ(λ) does the same for X − μ.

Claim · Differentiation Recovers Moments

When the MGF is finite near zero, differentiating k times and evaluating at zero gives

M(k)(0)=E[Xk]

Explanation of the Claim

In the power series, taking k derivatives removes all lower-order terms. At λ = 0, the higher-order terms vanish, leaving only E[Xᵏ]. Finiteness in a neighborhood of zero justifies these differentiations.

Example · The First Two Derivatives

M′(0)=E[X],M″(0)=E[X2]

For the centered MGF, ϕ′(0) = 0 and ϕ″(0) = Var(X). Thus its curvature at zero records the variance.

Insight · Why Exponential Moments Control Tails

For λ > 0, large positive deviations become large values of e^{λ(X−μ)}. If the expectation of this exponential remains small, large deviations cannot occur too often. The parameter λ controls how strongly we amplify them.
