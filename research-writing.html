It has been six or seven years since my first experience of research writing, and about ten years since I began exploring research. Looking back, it has been a difficult journey. Much as I wish I could forget all the pain and the feeling of being misunderstood, I can still remember how I felt then: the feeling of being lost, and the hope that time would help me find my way. That hope kept me exploring and thinking through failure after failure. Along the way, I completed my PhD, and I am now doing my postdoctoral work in an open and free environment, which I deeply appreciate and cherish.

Over the past year or two, I have also gradually come to recognize the limits of my own abilities. Yet I do not find this discouraging. What we can achieve is often limited by many external circumstances, even when we do our best. So I have begun spending more time helping the people around me whom I enjoy working with, hoping that our time working together can help them understand the essence of more things, think more, know themselves, enjoy life, and, with these as a foundation, pursue their own careers. Seeing my collaborators publish papers I find meaningful or find PhD positions they had hoped for has been deeply fulfilling for me.

I am writing this post in the hope that whoever happens to come across it might find something useful in my writing, imperfect though it is. I want to share some of the thinking that has accompanied me, and perhaps encourage new thoughts of your own. If it helps someone feel a little less lost in research and approach their work more professionally, it will have served its purpose. I also hope that, when you open this post, you will treat it as a small, unexpected discovery. Please keep it to yourself, rather than sending it to me or sharing it with the people around you. I also hope that, when I reopen this post many years from now, I will have stayed true to the intentions behind these words, wherever my pursuits may have led me.

After thinking about where to begin, I decided to start with something I know well and that might be most useful to you: writing research papers. We will move from developing an idea to organizing a paper, then to the details that deserve attention during writing. Finally, we will discuss longer-term personal development: the thinking we need to cultivate and the ways we can continue to improve.

What Is a Research Idea?

I have noticed that many people who are just getting started in research have misconceptions about what a research idea is. Adding something to a learning objective or a model architecture, and assuming, based on intuition alone, that it will improve experimental performance, does not constitute a complete research idea—even if it actually improves performance. If someone who has worked with me for a long time still presents something like this to me as a research idea, I would feel deeply saddened.

A complete research idea tells a coherent story. There is a background, practical significance, previous efforts to address the problem, and limitations in those efforts. There is your analysis of those limitations, the thinking that follows from that analysis, and a new method motivated by that thinking. An idea is usually a single point, but that point needs the support of everything above.

Everyone’s ideas start out simple and immature, and developing them usually takes a great deal of time and thought. I may spend months letting an idea take shape in my mind, and sometimes return to ideas I abandoned years ago. But if you think along these lines and gradually build a complete structure in your mind, the process is useful both for the practical goal of getting a paper accepted and for the sincere desire to share your thinking.

It is also important to remember that logic is at the heart of an idea. This is why, after hearing what a collaborator wants to do, I sometimes say “reasonable,” and sometimes hold my head in frustration because I cannot follow the logic of the story being told.

What matters is that the reasoning flows clearly and that the claims can be checked. The connection between each claim and the next should be clear and easy to follow, and the claims on which the argument rests should be verifiable, whether you plan to test them directly through experiments or theory, or justify them by citing some previous papers.

Having several points is fine, too. But it is usually necessary to distinguish the main idea from the supporting ones and organize the writing around the main idea. Otherwise, the paper becomes difficult to write and difficult for readers to follow. (You are trying to tell them something interesting, not leave them feeling confused or frustrated, right?) From this perspective, there is still one central point, with the others serving as extensions or further discussion.

This also helps explain why you often see papers built around simple ideas get accepted. Although there is considerable randomness in the review process, these papers with high probability tell a complete and convincing story. In my view, the quality of this whole story determines whether a researcher is competent. Beyond that, the choice of research direction—what field to work in—is what distinguishes the very best researchers from ordinary ones like us. This is a matter of research taste, although luck also plays an important role.

The only thing we can do is read more papers across different fields. But it is a mistake to approach them like a high school student who takes everything as truth. After all, many papers have problems, not to mention those written for questionable reasons and published for reasons that are hard to understand. However, engaging critically with their ideas across time and space is what makes research so enjoyable.

So we need to learn to distinguish good work from poor work. A paper from a leading research institute, or with a famous name among its authors, is not automatically a good paper. Nor do you have to consider a paper good just because everyone else does. Judge it for yourself and think about it.

To me, a good paper either offers a thorough and insightful discussion of the background and related work, helping me become familiar with a field I may not have known before, or has thoughtful ideas behind it. What matters most is understanding those ideas, finding even a few words that inspire your own thinking, or gaining a new understanding of something.

Read more. Judge more. Think more. Reading and thinking more are always worthwhile.

How to Write a Paragraph

Before discussing how to start writing and organize a paper (once the idea is clear, writing the paper becomes much easier), let’s first consider a more basic question: how do we write a paragraph?

First, I have nothing against using GPT or Claude to help with writing. This post itself is being written with GPT. But at least for now, GPT cannot infer your reasoning from nothing or from a few scattered remarks. Even when you give it the whole story, it often produces writing that looks correct on the surface but is hollow underneath, and sometimes even misleads you.

I have tried taking a shortcut by asking GPT to write a first draft, intending to revise the logic and wording afterward. Instead, I was completely misled by it and spent even more time figuring out what was wrong. In my judgment, GPT cannot yet handle the complexity of an argument across an entire paper. It is better suited to helping you develop the text when you provide the specific reasoning, or discussing a concrete question with you. Even then, its writing is far from reliable. Many logical problems remain, and you need to read it repeatedly.

When I wrote my first paper, without GPT, I probably read and revised each paragraph hundreds of times. With GPT, that much repetition may no longer be necessary, but it is still worth spending a day examining the reasoning: is the logic crystal clear, and do the transitions between paragraphs flow naturally? This requires repeated reading and thinking, both from the reader’s perspective and from that of a professional researcher striving for precision and rigor. Looking only at metrics such as AI-detection scores is a form of self-deception. It offers a false sense of assurance.

We want readers to understand what we mean without difficulty, while also expressing our own distinctive thinking, perhaps even our philosophy. The simplest and most common way to organize a paragraph is to begin with the main point and then develop it. The first sentence usually connects to the previous paragraph and makes clear what this paragraph will do. The sentences that follow should serve that purpose, with reasoning that flows clearly and smoothly. As the paragraph draws to a close, try introducing a new point that leads into what follows.

There are also a few additional issues I would like to discuss here.

First, there is a difference between logical leaps (逻辑跳) and a lack of logical focus (逻辑散). Some people confuse the two, perhaps because of how they are expressed in Chinese, but they are fundamentally different. The distinction is a little easier to understand in English.

Logical leaps mean that the underlying logic is there, but its presentation is not quite smooth. This is like a mathematical proof in which someone extraordinarily clever or with strong intuition skips a few steps: the conclusion can still be rigorously justified, even though the reader needs those missing steps to follow the reasoning.

A lack of logical focus is a more serious problem. It means that you do not really know what the current discussion is meant to accomplish or which specific claim it is meant to support. This is like listing facts that appear correct in a mathematical proof, without knowing what conclusion you intend to establish or being able to derive a sound conclusion from them. Knowing exactly what you are doing is essential.

Second, I want to discuss the difference between a lack of information and high information entropy. Both are serious problems, and neither seems to receive enough attention. Usually, I rely on intuition to judge whether each sentence is as good as it can be, and use that judgment to polish the paper. But when I am really unsure or trying to explain a problem to a collaborator, apart from flawed reasoning or a lack of justification, my most common diagnosis is that the sentence provides no useful information or has too much entropy.

By a lack of information, I mean that the sentence does not support your argument and only makes things more complicated. Even if it contains something useful, it may still place an unnecessary burden on the reader. If you really want to keep it, the best approach is to find a separate place to discuss it later in the paper or in an appendix.

By high information entropy, I mean that a sentence is ambiguous, either in its wording or in its structure. For example, in a passage I read recently about agents, the word “update” was used without specifying what was being updated. But in the literature discussed in that passage, the harness, memory, and even the LLM can all be updated. “Update” needs further qualification, such as “LLM update,” to make its meaning clear. Eliminating this kind of ambiguity is an important goal of repeated reading.

A final, relatively less important point concerns word choice. Beyond ambiguity in terminology, there are many other details to examine: adverbs that qualify a claim accurately, appropriate connectives, and even the implications of the verbs themselves. I used to look up synonyms for a verb and repeatedly consider the subtle differences between them before choosing the most suitable one. I remember that, a long time ago, I would often spend an entire afternoon thinking about which word to use. That habit has stayed with me. I still often challenge GPT about whether a particular word or sentence implies something I do not intend to claim.

Terminology should also be defined when it first appears. This is an important convention in scientific and technical writing. Your readers may come from different fields and may not know everything that is familiar to people in your area. A word may also mean different things in different fields. It is your responsibility to remove that ambiguity. From the perspective of making the paper self-contained, you should also define every term you use. Whether the work is theoretical or engineering-oriented, your goal should be to make the paper understandable with as little effort as possible, even for readers new to the field.

Consistency in wording also matters, especially for terms you have already defined. Do not keep switching between different names. Once something has been precisely defined or discussed, its terminology should remain consistent throughout the rest of the paper. Otherwise, the writing falls short of the rigor it should maintain.
