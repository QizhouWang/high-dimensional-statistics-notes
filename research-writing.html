SECTION 01Build the argument before polishing the sentences

I think about research writing at two timescales. In the short term, we need to turn a particular piece of work into a clear, coherent paper. In the long term, we need to develop the thinking and judgment that make such a paper possible. This post moves from the overall structure to the details of writing, then to the habits behind them.

An idea is only part of the story. A paper also needs to explain the problem, its significance, the limitations of existing approaches, the reasoning behind the proposed solution, and the evidence supporting its conclusions. By “story,” I mean this connected argument.

Before writing individual sections, try answering five questions:

  1. What problem are we trying to solve, and why does it matter?
  2. What have previous studies achieved, and what remains unresolved?
  3. What does our analysis suggest about this limitation?
  4. How does our method follow from that analysis?
  5. What evidence supports our claims, and where do those claims stop?

The central connection is between the analysis and the method. The reader should understand why the proposed method is a reasonable response to the problem we identified. If that connection is missing, the paper may contain a motivation, a method, and several results without a convincing argument linking them.

Suppose we argue that a method fails because it relies on unreliable information. A proposed solution that filters this information should explain how the filter identifies the relevant failures. The evaluation should then examine whether filtering addresses those failures, alongside any overall performance improvement.

A useful starting point is a short outline: background, analysis, method, and evidence. Write the main claim of each part in one or two sentences. If you cannot explain the connections at this level, more polished prose is unlikely to resolve the difficulty.

SECTION 02Give each section a clear purpose

Once the argument is clear, decide how each section will help the reader follow it. The exact structure depends on the work, but each section should have a recognizable purpose.

Abstract and introduction

The abstract presents a compact version of the whole argument: the problem, the central idea, and the main finding. It should make clear what the paper contributes without requiring the reader to reconstruct the motivation.

The introduction develops the same argument with more context. Explain why the problem matters, what remains difficult, and how this work makes progress. The abstract and introduction should agree on the central message, while using their different amounts of space effectively.

Related work and preliminaries

Related work helps readers locate the contribution. Explain which studies address similar questions, how their assumptions or approaches differ, and what this paper adds. Describe previous work accurately; a contribution is more credible when its relationship to existing work is precise.

Preliminaries provide the definitions, notation, and background needed for what follows. Include enough for the intended reader to understand the argument, and introduce specialized material near the point where it becomes useful.

Method and evidence

The method section should explain both what we do and why we do it. Give the reader the intuition needed to understand the construction, then the details needed to examine or reproduce it.

Organize experiments and theoretical results around the questions they answer. For each result, explain the claim being examined, why the setup is appropriate, and what can be concluded. A collection of results becomes persuasive when the reader understands their roles in the argument.

Performance matters in relation to the goal of the work. If the main claim is better predictive performance, weak performance is a serious problem. If the claim concerns efficiency, robustness, or understanding, the evidence needs to establish that contribution and make the relevant tradeoffs clear.

SECTION 03Move from paragraphs to sentences

After the structure is in place, work through the paper at progressively smaller scales. First check how sections connect, then how paragraphs develop, and finally how individual sentences read.

Paragraphs should advance the argument

Each paragraph should have a clear task: introduce a problem, explain a limitation, develop an insight, or interpret a result. Its sentences should work together toward that task. At the end of the paragraph, the reader should understand something that helps them follow the next step.

Check the transitions by asking why one paragraph follows another. Words such as “therefore” and “however” are useful only when the underlying logical relationship is present. If the connection is difficult to explain, reconsider the order or supply the missing reasoning.

Sentences should make their meaning explicit

Use clear subjects and specific references. Expressions such as “this,” “it,” and “the above” should have an obvious referent. Keep qualifications close to the claims they qualify, and separate sentences when they carry too many distinct ideas.

Remove repetition and phrases that contribute little information. Concision should preserve the reasoning: sometimes an extra sentence explaining a necessary connection makes a paper much easier to read.

Terminology and definitions should stay consistent

Use the same term for the same concept. Define specialized terms and symbols before relying on them, and distinguish concepts that play different roles. Readers should be able to follow the main argument using the definitions and explanations in the paper, with references providing further background where needed.

Be especially careful with the strength of a claim. An observation, a hypothesis, and an established conclusion carry different commitments. State which one you mean, and keep the scope of the wording aligned with the scope of the evidence.

A practical revision checklist

  • Does each section contribute to the central argument?
  • Does each paragraph have a clear purpose and a logical connection to the next?
  • Are terms, symbols, and references defined and used consistently?
  • Can any sentence be simplified without losing necessary reasoning?
  • Are the claims supported, appropriately qualified, and limited to what was examined?

SECTION 04Use revision to examine your thinking

Difficulty writing can reveal difficulty understanding the work. If we cannot state the contribution clearly, perhaps we have not yet identified it precisely. If we cannot explain why the method follows from the motivation, perhaps that connection needs more thought. If a result is difficult to interpret, perhaps the experiment does not answer the question we intended to ask.

Revision therefore involves checking both the expression and the reasoning. Ask whether a confusing passage needs clearer wording, a missing explanation, or a change to the underlying argument. These problems require different responses.

Critical thinking also means examining our own preferred explanations. What assumptions does the argument rely on? What alternative explanation could account for the result? What evidence would lead us to revise the claim? These questions help us decide what to test and how strongly to state the conclusion.

Careful checking is part of this process. Verify that the text, equations, figures, and experimental descriptions agree. Small inconsistencies can leave the reader unsure about what was actually done or what a result means.

Writing can thus feed back into research. A draft may expose a missing comparison, an unclear assumption, or an unsupported inference. Following up on those gaps can improve the work itself.

SECTION 05Develop independent judgment over time

A checklist can improve a particular draft. Becoming a better researcher and writer requires repeated practice in reading, judging, and thinking.

Read with attention to the argument

When reading a paper, examine how it is constructed. How does it establish the problem? What connects the motivation to the method? Which results carry the main conclusion? Where does the explanation become difficult to follow?

Read beyond the immediate task when possible. Broader reading can help us recognize recurring questions, alternative ways to formulate a problem, and assumptions that our own area takes for granted.

Form your own assessment

After reading, try writing a short assessment in your own words. State the contribution, the strongest evidence, and the main uncertainty. Distinguish what the paper demonstrates from what it suggests.

Then compare your assessment with those of collaborators or other readers. Understand the reasons for any disagreement. Over time, this helps you develop standards you can explain and revise.

Make room for reflection

Think about why you choose particular problems, what you consider meaningful progress, and what evidence you find convincing. A research philosophy can begin with these concrete judgments and develop as experience changes them.

In daily work, coding, reading, writing, and thinking can inform one another. Writing may clarify what to implement; an experiment may change the argument; reading may reveal a better question. Making room for reflection helps these activities connect.

Learn from each revision

When a collaborator improves a draft, examine what changed and why. Did they repair a logical gap, narrow an overstatement, move a definition, or remove repetition? Understanding the reason makes the lesson useful in the next paper.

The long-term aim is to become more capable of recognizing and addressing these problems yourself: deciding what matters, building a coherent argument, checking it carefully, and expressing it clearly enough for others to examine.
